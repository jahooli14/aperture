For Katie · a worked example

Four things you get to say this month

Two hundred meeting notes across six months — invented, but the kind you'd actually write after an hour with a CUO. Plus the diary. Everything below came out of reading all two hundred at once, which is the one thing nobody ever does.

Two of them are tests. Have a go before you scroll past.

Fictitious clients and colleagues throughout — none of these are your accounts. Every quotation is checked word-for-word against the note it cites; a bracketed letter marks the one permitted edit, capitalising a phrase lifted from mid-sentence.

Test one

Three clients, three firms, one view of where the market's going.

Five weeks apart. Nobody prompted any of them.

10 JUN Piotr Zawada · Bellwether MGA 12 May breakfast

“[Y]our report says half the managing agents disappear by 2030. If that's right our capacity panel looks very different in five years.”

24 JUN Ivan Kroll · Pemberton Broking 12 May breakfast

Repeats the same consolidation number back. Attributes it to “everyone”.

15 JUL Tobias Greer · Orwell Marine on the mailing list

“[Y]ou lot are saying half the managing agents go by 2030.”

Three independent sources. Is the market converging on this?

No. It's your own number, coming back.

Your firm published it on 14 April. All three were sent the report; two were in the room when it was presented on 12 May. What you wrote in your own note that day:

14 APR Your note, the day it published

“I have no idea whether that's true. Nobody I've asked internally knows where the number came from.”

By the third telling it has stopped being a report. Ivan didn't say he'd read it — he said everyone was saying it. An unsourced number of yours is now circulating as market consensus, and the next place it lands is a partner's slide, cited as what clients are telling us.

What you get to say

“Careful with that number — it's ours, and nobody in my firm can tell me where it came from. Let me tell you what I'm actually hearing instead.”

Nobody else in that room can say it. It costs you nothing and buys you the rest of the meeting.

Test two

Two chief underwriting officers, six weeks apart, flatly contradicting each other.

Both senior, both have seen three cycles. Neither knows the other said it.

19 MAR Marcus Deyn · Ravensbourne, CUO

“The data's finally good enough that we can write parametric on things that aren't windstorm. I've got a team on it.”

5 MAY Ana Castellane · Harrow Point, Head of Specialty

“Parametric is a solution looking for a problem … The problem is the buyer thinks they bought insurance and they bought a bet.”

Which of them is right?

Both. And you've already met the two people who settle it.

Buried in two buyer conversations, four months apart, filed under different accounts:

15 MAY Graham Tull · Duraflex, a buyer proves Ana right

“I've got a real problem, I don't need a clever one.”

25 AUG Beatrice Ofori · Norton Hale, a buyer proves Marcus right

Puts port delay at £4m last year, none of it insured.

“If someone can pay me when the port backs up I don't care what they call it.”

The variable is whether the buyer already counts the loss. Beatrice has a figure for hers and no cover against it — a payout that arrives on a trigger is strictly better than the nothing she gets now. Graham doesn't have a figure, so a trigger is just a new way to be disappointed. The disagreement was never the finding. The rule underneath it is.

What you get to say

“Parametric lands where the buyer already counts the loss. I know a Head of Risk carrying £4m a year of port delay with nothing against it. Shall I introduce you?”

To Marcus, who has been asking for buyer introductions since May.

What you get to say

“Klaus Beringer called this in April, when he told me almost nobody was saying it. You're the fifth person to tell me since, and none of you have spoken to each other.”

The useful part isn't the call. It's that you now know whose read to weight next time — and four people have just learned you're the person who keeps score.

The one nobody can do

Something stopped. Something else started. Both were silent.

The June reporting deadline was in every other conversation in the spring. Then it wasn't — and nobody announces that they've stopped caring about something.

Meanwhile, quietly, across the same six months — five clients described the same cost. A carrier, two MGAs, a mutual and a broker. Not one of them used a word any of the others used:

  • MGA · 6 Apr“Three sets of homework.”
  • MGA · 28 Apr“[A] hundred and eighty thousand pounds … proving that we're allowed to do the thing we were already doing.”
  • Carrier · 3 Jun“[A] seven figure sum a year to be able to say we looked.”
  • Mutual · 16 Jun“[F]orty binders and three people looking after them.”
  • Broker · 6 Jul“Same coverholder. Three times over.”

No keyword joins those five. No search would have found them together. And they matter now because of the first half of this section: Nils in Regulatory hired three people for the June deadline in March. In June he told you “I need a second act and I don't have one.” As of last week they have nothing booked past October.

What you get to say

“Nobody's mentioned the June deadline since the first week of July. But five clients spent the spring telling me the same thing about the cost of binder oversight, in five different sets of words, and none of them know the others said it. There's your second act.”

To Nils, who asked you in June what you were hearing that was regulatory-shaped. The answer was already in your notes — it just wasn't in any one of them.

The harder half

What it saw and refused to tell you.

A broker's system migration

Five separate mentions since March — the single loudest topic in six months of notes.

Three people, one firm. That's an account, not a market. Stayed quiet.

The AI trend that wasn't one

Three clients in four weeks. One hated a vendor demo, one is building submission triage, one is running claims fraud detection.

Same word, three unrelated subjects. Stayed quiet.

Anything will find you patterns if you let it. The discipline is in the refusals — and on most weeks, honestly, the output is nothing at all.

Where it reaches you

Three days before, not the morning after.

Findings attach themselves to the diary. One line, carried in — never a summary of the last meeting.
