A worked example · specialty insurance

You've talked to everyone. You can only use what you remember.

That's the whole problem. Your value to a client is that you've spoken to everyone else in the market — but walking into any given meeting, you can deploy the fraction you happen to recall. Six months of conversations, and maybe the last three weeks are live in your head.

What it does for you

Reads all two hundred conversations, every week, and hands you the two or three worth knowing before you walk in — including things the client doesn't know about their own market.

What it does for the firm

Somewhere in these notes is a team of yours sitting idle with nothing booked, and five clients describing the exact problem it solves. Nobody spotted it, because nobody reads all the notes at once.

Below is that corpus. Every square is one note. Pick a pattern and watch which ones light up. No insurance knowledge needed — it's all explained in plain English as it comes.

And it reaches you three days early.

Findings attach themselves to your diary. Before the meeting, not the morning after — one line to carry in, never a summary of the last one.

The obvious objection

Why not just ask Claude to read your notes before the meeting?

For the meeting brief — you should. It works today, needs no system, and two hundred notes fit in a chat window with room to spare. If that were the whole idea, don't build anything.

The difference is everything that isn't about the meeting you're walking into:

  • 01

    You can't write a prompt for the thing you don't know to ask. Four of the five patterns above aren't about the person you're seeing. To get the first one out of a chat you'd have to type “is anything I'm being told actually my own firm's report coming back to me?” Nobody types that.

  • 02

    An absence has nothing to retrieve. You cannot look up the thing that stopped being said. It only exists as a gap between two months.

  • 03

    Asked to find patterns, anything will find some. Tested with the safeguards switched off, three of four deliberately planted traps fired — one firm's IT project read as an industry trend, and this firm's own report read as market consensus.

  • 04

    It happens whether or not you remember to do it. The manual version survives about three weeks. A scheduled one also knows what it told you last week, so it doesn't repeat itself until you stop reading.

None of this needs a cleverer model. It's a fixed set of questions, asked reliably, with a rule about when to say nothing.
