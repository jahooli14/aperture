A worked example

Four things nobody could have noticed

Two hundred meeting notes, six months, one business development person in specialty insurance. Invented, but the kind anyone in that job actually writes. Something read all two hundred at once — which is the one thing a person never does — and found four things.

You don't need to know anything about insurance to follow this. Every industry term is explained in plain English where it comes up. The four findings are all shapes you'd recognise from any job.

Fictitious clients, colleagues and firms throughout. Every quotation is checked word-for-word against the note it cites; a bracketed letter marks the one permitted edit, capitalising a phrase lifted from mid-sentence.

Everything it found is one of four shapes. None of them can be spotted from inside a single conversation — that's what they have in common.

01
The echo. Something you said, coming back months later as something you heard.
02
The contradiction. Two people you trust, flatly disagreeing — and the answer sitting in a third conversation neither was part of.
03
The first voice. Who said it before everyone else, and so whose judgement to back next time.
04
The silence. Something everyone was talking about that stopped. Nobody ever announces that.

01 · The echo

Three clients told you where the industry is heading.

Three different firms. Five weeks apart. Nobody prompted any of them.

In plain terms

All three are repeating the same bold prediction about the industry's future — that half the firms running Lloyd's underwriting syndicates will disappear by 2030. It's the kind of quotable claim that gets into strategy decks. You don't need to care whether it's true; watch where it came from.

Three independent sources. Is the industry converging on this?

No. It's your own number, coming back.

Your firm published that prediction on 14 April. All three of them were sent the report; two were in the room when you presented it in May. Here is what you wrote in your own notes the day it went out:

14 APRYour own note, the day it published

“I have no idea whether that's true. Nobody I've asked internally knows where the number came from.”

By the third telling it has stopped being a report. Ivan didn't say he'd read it — he said everyone was saying it. A number your own firm can't source is now circulating as what the industry thinks, and the next place it lands is a partner's slide, quoted as what clients are telling us.

What you get to say

“Careful with that number — it's ours, and nobody in my firm can tell me where it came from. Let me tell you what I'm actually hearing instead.”

Nobody else in that room can say it. It costs nothing and buys you the rest of the meeting.

02 · The contradiction

Two senior people told you the exact opposite thing.

Six weeks apart. Both run underwriting at large insurers, both have seen three cycles. Neither knows the other said it.

In plain terms

They're arguing about parametric cover — insurance that pays out automatically when a measurable thing happens (the port backs up, the wind passes 80mph) rather than after an assessor works out what you actually lost. It's faster and cheaper to run. The catch: it can pay you nothing on a day you really did lose money.

19 MARMarcus Deyn· runs underwriting at an insurer

“The data's finally good enough that we can write parametric on things that aren't windstorm. I've got a team on it.”

5 MAYAna Castellane· runs specialty at another insurer

“Parametric is a solution looking for a problem … The problem is the buyer thinks they bought insurance and they bought a bet.”

Which of them is right?

Both. And you've already met the two people who settle it.

Not other underwriters — two customers, in conversations four months apart, filed under completely different accounts:

15 MAYGraham Tull· buys insurance for a manufacturerAna is right about him

“I've got a real problem, I don't need a clever one.”

25 AUGBeatrice Ofori· buys insurance for a logistics firmMarcus is right about her

Her ports were delayed so often last year it cost £4m — none of it insured.

“If someone can pay me when the port backs up I don't care what they call it.”

The deciding factor is whether the customer already knows what the problem costs them. Beatrice has a number — £4m a year — and nothing protecting it, so an automatic payout beats the nothing she gets today. Graham has no number, so an automatic payout is just a new way to be let down. The disagreement was never the finding. The rule underneath it is, and it took four conversations across five months to see.

What you get to say

“This works where the customer already knows what the problem costs them. I know a head of risk losing £4m a year to port delays with nothing against it. Shall I introduce you?”

To Marcus, who has been asking you for customer introductions since May.

03 · The first voice

You're the fifth person to be told this. Klaus was the first.

Not one of the later four mentions him. Each describes it as something happening in their own business, in their own words.

In plain terms

They're all describing the same thing: injury and liability claims are getting dramatically more expensive, faster than anyone priced for. Klaus saw it first because he reinsures dozens of companies and sees the total picture. The others each see only their own slice — so to them it looks like a local problem.

What you get to say

“Klaus Beringer called this in April, when he told me almost nobody was saying it. You're the fifth person to tell me since, and none of you have spoken to each other.”

The useful part isn't the prediction. It's that you now know whose judgement to back — and four people have just learned you're the one keeping score.

04 · The silence

Something stopped. Something else started. Both were silent.

A regulatory deadline in June was in nearly every conversation through the spring. Then it wasn't — and nobody ever announces that they've stopped caring about something.

Meanwhile, quietly, over the same six months — five clients described the same expensive problem. Not one used a word any of the others used.

In plain terms

Insurers let other firms sell policies on their behalf. Each insurer then audits each of those firms separately, forever — the same firm, the same questions, several times over, in different formats. Everyone involved thinks it costs far more than it ever catches. It's compliance theatre, and it's expensive.

  • 6 Apr“Three sets of homework.”
  • 28 Apr“[A] hundred and eighty thousand pounds … proving that we're allowed to do the thing we were already doing.”
  • 3 Jun“[A] seven figure sum a year to be able to say we looked.”
  • 16 Jun“[F]orty binders and three people looking after them.”
  • 6 Jul“Same coverholder. Three times over.”

Five people, same complaint, no shared words. No search finds those together — you'd have to already know they were the same thing. And it matters now because of the first half: Nils in your regulatory team hired three people for the June deadline. In June he told you “I need a second act and I don't have one.” As of last week they have nothing booked past October.

What you get to say

“Nobody's mentioned the June deadline since the first week of July. But five clients spent the spring describing the same expensive problem, in five completely different sets of words, and none of them know the others said it. There's your second act.”

To Nils, who asked you in June what you were hearing. The answer was already in your notes — it just wasn't in any one of them.

The harder half

What it saw and refused to tell you.

A broker's IT project

Five separate mentions since March — the single loudest topic in six months of notes.

Three people, all at one firm. That's one account, not an industry trend. Stayed quiet.

The AI trend that wasn't one

Three clients in four weeks. One hated a sales demo, one is sorting incoming enquiries, one is catching fraud.

Same word, three unrelated subjects. Stayed quiet.

Anything will find you patterns if you let it. The discipline is in the refusals — and most weeks, honestly, the output is nothing at all.

Where it reaches you

Three days before, not the morning after.

Findings attach themselves to the diary. One line to carry in — never a summary of the last meeting.
